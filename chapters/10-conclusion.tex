\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{The case}\label{sec:case}
Returns cost retailers hundreds of billions of dollars a year, with estimates from \usd{685} to \usd{890} billion for 2024~\cite{appriss2024,nrf2024}, and size and fit uncertainty is a leading cause~\cite{coresight2023}. ClosetTwin's answer, a fit tool built on a body scan, garment data and stated preference, is a hypothesis and not a result. A four-week, shopper-level pilot can accept or reject it cheaply, and a retailer breaks even at an effect of 4.5\% to 6.0\%, below our 10\% pass line.

\section{Israel first, the ask and next steps}\label{sec:ask}
Israel's addressable fee pool is only about \usd{198,000} a year, so it cannot pay for the company. We choose it as a reference market because we can reach all 30 candidate retailers directly, pilots there are cheap, and a passed pilot at a large local retailer is the evidence a European buyer would ask for. That is an argument, not a proven fact. Europe carries the revenue and enters in year 4.

We ask for about \derived{\usd{3.9}} million (\usd{3,898,650}): the cumulative deficit of \usd{3.25} million at the end of year 5 plus a 20\% buffer. It funds the plan through year 5 only. Run-rate break-even needs about 44 European paying clients, and the base case has 18 at the end of year 5, so break-even lies outside the five-year model. Table~\ref{tab:company-sens} shows how the result moves under other assumptions. We propose to release the money in tranches tied to the pilot gate: the first for the Israeli pilots, the second for Europe only after a large-retailer pilot passes the 10\% line. The plan shows no operating profit within five years. If no large-retailer pilot passes, we stop and do not scale.
