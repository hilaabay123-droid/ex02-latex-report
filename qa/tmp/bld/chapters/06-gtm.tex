\chapter{Go-to-Market}\label{ch:gtm}

\section{Segments and channels}\label{sec:segments}
ClosetTwin enters Israel first and sells directly to the 30 large and mid-size local fashion e-tailers (5 large and 25 mid-size, both counts \assumed{assumed}). The plan names no Israeli retailer as a client. The only retailer-level sizes we found are order shares for all categories, not fashion: the 2020 ranking of the largest Israeli online retailers is led by Amazon, followed by Shein, KSP and Next, and a November 2024 chart in the same report shows order shares of several local sites, among them Terminal X at 12\%~\cite{knesset_ric2025}. Neither gives the online fashion sales of a local fashion e-tailer, so the counts of 5 large and 25 mid-size retailers and their order volumes stay assumptions. The low-touch route through an app store is weak in Israel. One third-party tracker counts about 4\% of .il shops on Shopify and flags its own page as low confidence~\cite{aguko_shopify_il}, and a Shopify Plus agency opened locally only in 2024~\cite{globes2024shopify}. We therefore reserve the Shopify App Store route~\cite{shopify_dev_build} for the Europe stage, which starts in year 4. A route into Europe through Israeli retailers that already sell to the EU is a plausible way to use Israeli references, but the model does not include it: its European clients are reached by direct contacts (Table~\ref{tab:funnel}). The largest Israeli fashion sites are foreign~\cite{knesset_ric2025} and sit outside our addressable market.

\section{The funnel}\label{sec:funnel}
Table~\ref{tab:funnel} turns the assumptions into a year-by-year path. In Israel, 17 of the 30 candidates become pilots over five years (57\%), and about 6 are paying clients in year 5. Every conversion rate is \assumed{assumed}. Because the pool is small and the market is warm, we use contact-to-meeting 60\% and meeting-to-pilot 30\%, so 18\% of contacts become pilots. In Europe we use 30\% and 30\% (9\%). The sales team is never the bottleneck: contacts needed use at most 41\% of capacity, where capacity is 25\% of headcount times 12 contacts a week over 46 weeks. The weak link is not capacity but the pilot-to-paying conversion. We assume 50\% for Europe, with no source and no published benchmark behind it. The conversion has two parts, the chance that a pilot passes the gate and the chance that a passing client buys, and neither can be known before the first pilots finish. The Israeli path is lower: 17 pilots lead to about 6 paying clients, roughly \derived{35\%}, because we set those client counts by hand and capped them. The same rule is not applied to both markets, and the company sensitivity of Chapter~\ref{ch:finance} shows the plan at 35\% for Europe as well.

\section{The pilot design}\label{sec:pilot-design}
The claim to be tested is that a fit tool lowers the fit-related return rate, defined as orders with a size or fit return reason divided by orders. We randomize at shopper level, 50/50, and analyse by intention to treat (Table~\ref{tab:pilot}). With a significance level of 0.05 (two-sided) and 80\% power, a four-week pilot at a large Israeli retailer of 600,000 orders a year detects a relative reduction of about \derived{7.8\%}. At a European retailer of one million orders a year the figure is \derived{6.1\%}. The pass line is a 10\% reduction with a 95\% interval above zero, set above the retailer's break-even effect of 4.5\% to 6.0\% (Chapter~\ref{ch:finance}). A mid-size Israeli retailer of 100,000 orders a year could detect only about \derived{19\%}. A mid-size retailer also produces only about 3,850 orders per arm in four weeks, so the planned minimum of 12,000 orders per arm is met by large retailers only. Mid-size pilots therefore either run longer or count as pooled evidence, and only large-retailer pilots are individual tests. Pooled evidence needs its own design, with the pilots analysed together and the differences between retailers reported, and we have not yet fixed it. Of the 30 Israeli candidates, only the 5 large ones can run an individual test. In Europe, most pilots are free app-store installs that have no control group. We call their results non-causal, and we do not count them toward the pass line. The need for such a test is real: the largest study in our sources found that shoppers using a size finder were slightly more likely to return an item~\cite{patel2025}. If a retailer cannot randomize, a matched before-and-after comparison is the fallback, and we label it weaker evidence. Israeli pilots come from warm design-partner contacts and start only after the privacy compliance milestone of Chapter~\ref{ch:ops}.
