1.1 The returns bill
Every order that comes back is a sale that did not finish and a cost that did. The two best estimates of what this costs retailers in the United States are large and differ from each other. Appriss Retail reports that total returns of the retail industry amounted to $685 billion in merchandise in 2024, and the National Retail Federation projects $890 billion for the same year [1], [2]. The gap of $205 billion is a difference of method and not a contradiction. The Appriss figure is built from surveys of 150 North American retail executives and 1,000 consumers together with data of the US Census Bureau, while the NRF figure is a projection published on the federation’s report page [1], [2]. We therefore quote the range and not a single number, and we do not add the two. Appriss also reports that online sales account for $362 billion of the returned value and that fraud and abuse account for 15.14% of all returns [1]. The second figure matters for honesty: not every return is a fit problem, and a fitting tool cannot reduce fraud.
Two different return rates circulate in the trade press, and they must not be merged. Appriss reports that online returns reached 24.52% of all online sales, which covers every product category [1]. Coresight Research estimates that the average return rate of online apparel orders was 24.4% for the twelve months ended March 2023, from a survey of 100 decision-makers at US apparel brands and retailers [3]. The similarity of the two numbers is a coincidence of two scopes. This plan uses the Coresight figure, because it is the one that is specific to apparel, and it states each time that the base is a small US survey.
1.2 Why fit is the core cause
Among the reasons for apparel returns, size and fit come first. In the Coresight survey, 53% of respondents name size or fit as the top reason for online apparel returns, followed by color (16%) and damage (10%) [3]. This is a share of respondents and not a share of returns, and the plan treats it that way: our own model assumes that 40% of returns are fit-related, which is a deliberate step below the survey figure (Chapter 8). The same picture appears in the research literature. Abdulla et al. state that online shopping yields a large percentage of returns due to size and fit [5], and Alemany et al. describe the high number of returns and their management, logistics

The literature also tells us what kind of problem this is. Alemany et al. note that earlier fitting technologies did not reach a compromise between cost, efficacy and accuracy that would allow wide market penetration [6], and we treat this as the historical gap that a lower-cost scan and a data-driven model can address. In a different field, Pfaff and Spann report that augmented reality is particularly effective for larger products with higher spatial fit uncertainty [8]. We cite it only as an analogy: the study is about home interiors and not about apparel.
1.3 What one return costs
A return is expensive because it is handled twice. In the one study that built the cost of a return from transaction data, 2,229 return transactions of a Scandinavian fashion footwear retailer, the cost of returns is approximately 17% of the prime cost per pair sold, and product handling and transportation together make up 72% of the total cost [4]. The paper states this per pair sold, not per returned pair, and its overall return rate is 28% [4], so the cost of one returned pair is a higher share of the prime cost than 17%. Our model applies the 17% to each return, which gives $6.80 per return at the assumptions of Chapter 5; we treat this as a conservative lower bound, because a higher cost per return would raise the saving a retailer can expect. This is a single retailer and a single product category, so it is an anchor for the model and not a market constant. Coresight estimates, for the United States, that a 24.4% return rate on a $155.8 billion online apparel and footwear market translates to $38 billion in returns and $25.1 billion in processing costs in 2023 [3]. We use the Gustafsson ratio in our model and show the Coresight figures only as a statement of scale.
For Israel, which is the first market of this plan, the legal setting makes returns structurally material. According to the official guide of the Israeli Consumer Protection and Fair Trade Authority, a consumer may cancel a distance-selling transaction within fourteen days of closing the transaction or of receiving the document with its details, whichever is later; use of the product does not remove the right, and the business must refund the consumer within fourteen days of receiving the cancellation notice [9]. The right goes back to section 14C, which an amendment of 1998 added to the Consumer Protection Law and which already set a period of fourteen days [10]; later amendments changed the regime, so we rely on the Authority’s guide for the current rules. The business may charge a cancellation fee of 5% of the transaction or NIS 100,

1.4 The thesis and how this plan tests it
ClosetTwin is a business-to-business virtual fitting room for fashion e-commerce. The shopper gives a body scan from a phone, the retailer supplies the garment’s measurements, and the service combines both with the shopper’s stated fit preference to recommend a size. The claim at the center of the plan is a hypothesis and not a fact: a fit tool built on scan, garment data and preference lowers fit-related returns enough to pay for itself. The evidence is mixed. Gustafsson et al. model reductions of fit-related return costs of up to 80% under assumed verification rates, but this is a scenario [4]. The largest field study in our sources, covering 496,365 items ordered by 75,707 customers, found that customers who used a size finder were 0.65% more likely to return an item, although their customer lifetime value was higher [11]. A plan that ignored the second result would be dishonest.
The plan therefore treats the effect as the quantity to be measured. Chapter 2 sizes the market and finds that Israel alone is too small to carry the company. Chapter 3 places ClosetTwin among the existing vendors. Chapters 4 and 5 describe the product and its pricing, which is set at a fraction of the value a retailer saves if the hypothesis holds. Chapter 6 designs a randomized pilot that can accept or reject the hypothesis, and Chapters 8 to 10 show what the company costs, what it earns and what it asks for if the pilot passes. 2.1 Method and its limits
We size the market bottom-up, in orders and in the fee that ClosetTwin would earn on them, and we call the result a fee pool and not a market size. A fee pool is the number of orders a segment could send through the service multiplied by our blended price, so it measures what we could earn and not what retailers or shoppers spend. Each input carries one of three marks: sourced, derived by us from other inputs, or assumed by us with a stated basis (S sourced D derived A assumption). Table 2.1 lists them all.

4.2 From scan to body model
A body model built from scan data is feasible and well studied, and the studies explain why it is needed. Alhassawi et al. analyzed a dataset of 677 participants and found that only 63 of them (9.15%) kept the same size across bust, waist and hip, that 614 (90.84%) fell into different sizes on different measurements, and that 35.45% were not adequately accommodated by the existing sizing scheme [24]. In other words, a single size label fits a minority of bodies on all dimensions at once. Alemany et al. made the same point earlier, and noted that body-scanning technologies at that time had not achieved a compromise between cost, efficacy and accuracy that would allow wide market penetration [6]. That study is from 2013 and we cite it as history. What has changed is that phone-based capture now exists: 3DLOOK states that a user can complete a scan independently with two guided smartphone photos and no dedicated hardware [18]. This is a vendor statement, and it does not tell us how accurate such a scan is for apparel. We found no independent source for the accuracy of phone scans in apparel sizing, so we state it as an engineering target that the pilot must measure and not as a result.
4.3 Why scan alone is not enough
A body measurement does not choose a size, because the same body can want different sizes in different garments. Peng and Duraffourg ran an experiment with 175 female participants of shapes from XS to 4XL on six garment tops. They report that fit preference has a significant impact on the size choice, and that knowing the three-dimensional shape of customers alone is not enough for an efficient size recommendation tool [7]. Two further findings, which we know only from their abstracts, point the same way. Sembium et al. report that latent-factor models that use personas and return codes improve the area under the curve by 17 to 21% against baselines, and that in an online A/B test they raised the percentage of “fit” transactions by 0.49% over the control [25]. Zhang et al. report that thin models dissuade consumers with larger clothing sizes from buying online because of perceived body-size dissimilarity and a heightened perception of fit risk [26]. The product therefore takes three inputs and not one: the scan, the garment’s measurements and the shopper’s stated preference. That the combination beats any single input is a hypothesis of this plan, which the pilot tests (Chapter 6).
4.4 The size model and its accuracy limits
The best evidence we hold for the model comes from a study on scan data that used the same kind of measurements. Alhassawi et al. report that a support vector machine that works on the primary measurements reaches an accuracy of 89.66%, outperforming a model that adds further dimensions through principal-component

ClosetTwin

analysis, which reaches 68.97% [24]. They also report that sizes 32 and 34 received no correct predictions, which they attribute to overlapping features and too few training samples [24]. We take two lessons from this. First, a simple model on a few good measurements can be accurate enough to be worth testing. Second, the accuracy of such a model is lowest at the edges of the size range, so rare sizes need a fallback, for example a prompt to measure again or a note to the shopper that the recommendation is uncertain. We state the 89.66% as the best evidence in hand and not as ClosetTwin’s measured performance, because the study did not use our scans, our garments or our shoppers, and we have no measurement of our own yet.
4.5 Integration with retailer platforms
Integration follows a standard pattern. On Shopify, an app is a set of components that the developer declares in the app configuration, and Shopify runs most of them, so that only the backend is the developer’s to host [27]. A ClosetTwin app on that model needs a widget extension for the product page, a backend for the size model and a feed of the retailer’s garment data. We have checked this pattern only for Shopify, which is a minor channel in Israel (Chapter 6); other platforms are not yet checked, and the plan does not assume that integration there is equally cheap. We assume $0.03A as the cost of one scan in our own infrastructure, an engineering target that we have not measured, and 25%A of orders that include a scan, an adoption guess; together they give a cost to serve of $0.0075 per order (Chapter 5).
4.6 Privacy by design: scan for fit, not identification
A body scan is personal data, and the product is designed so that it is not used for identification. Israel’s Privacy Protection Law was amended by Amendment 13, published on 14 August 2024, and the amendment takes effect one year after publication, that is, on 14 August 2025 [28]. Under the law’s definitions, a biometric identifier is data of special sensitivity when it is used, or intended to be used, to identify a person or to verify his or her identity in a digital manner [28], [29]. ClosetTwin scans for fit only. The design therefore never uses body data to identify anyone, collects the minimum that the size model needs, keeps the scan separate from any account identity and, as a design rule, discards the images once the measurements are extracted. We offer explicit consent as our own design choice: the law defines consent as informed consent, explicit or implied [29], so we do not claim that the law requires it. Whether body measurements for sizing could count as health data, which is a separate category of special sensitivity [29], is an open legal question. We treat it as a design risk that the compliance review must answer, not as a conclusion (Chapter 9).
The regulator’s own documents point the same way. The Privacy Protection Authority’s 2021 draft opinion, published for public hearing, states that collecting excess data beyond what is necessary is in itself an infringement of privacy [30], and its 2025

We report this as the Authority’s view. It is not evidence that retailers or shoppers pay more for privacy. The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is secondary for this plan; it applies when Israeli sites sell to EU customers and in the Europe stage, and it treats biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a person as a special category [32].
4.7 Visualization: an extension, not the core
Image-based try-on is a separate research field that has been surveyed. Chen et al. reviewed 69 research articles on the factors that influence purchasing decisions and the adoption of virtual try-on [33], Islam et al. state that the technology has the potential to reduce returns and to optimize conversion [34], and Song et al. survey methods that synthesize a person image dressed in a given garment [35], and Ghodhbani et al. survey virtual try-on outfits more broadly [36]. The word to keep from this is “potential”: we know these studies only by their abstracts, and none of them shows a measured reduction in returns. The first release of ClosetTwin therefore leaves visualization out. If the pilot succeeds, a try-on image can be added as an extension on the same data, and Google’s try-on model, which we discuss in Chapter 3, shows that this layer is the one most exposed to platform competition.

Business Model and Pricing

5.1 Who pays for what
The retailer pays, and it pays per order served. In the model, an order is served when it is placed in a paying client’s shop, whether or not the shopper used the widget; Table 5.1 states this billing base. If only scanned orders were billed, revenue per client would fall to about a quarterD of the modelled figure at the assumed 25% scan share. The shopper uses the widget for free. This follows from where the benefit lands: the saving that ClosetTwin promises is the cost of the returns that no longer happen, and that saving grows with the number of orders the retailer processes. A fee per order therefore moves with the value delivered, it lets a retailer start small, and it avoids a fixed license that a retailer would have to justify before it has seen any effect. The only other revenue in the model is the fixed fee for the pilot. The data feed described in Chapter 4 earns nothing in this plan.
5.2 Value-based price
The price is set against the value, and the value is the return cost that the tool avoids. We follow the logic of Gustafsson et al., who compute what a fitting technology may cost per unit before it stops paying for itself. In their case study of a footwear retailer, with a prime cost assumed at EUR 30, the allowed cost is EUR 1.1 per pair if the technology verifies fit in 40% of cases, EUR 1.6 at 60% and EUR 2.1 at 80% [4]. Those figures belong to one retailer and one category, so we rebuild the calculation with our own inputs; formula (8.1) in Chapter 8 gives it symbol by symbol. The source puts the cost of returns at 17%S of the prime cost per pair sold, at an overall return rate of 28% [4], so the cost of one returned pair is a higher share of the prime cost. We

nevertheless apply the 17% to each return, as a conservative lower bound: a higher cost per return would raise the saving and make the tool easier to justify. With an $80A order and a prime cost that we assume to be 50%A of it, the prime cost is $40D and a return costs $6.80D. The return rate is 24.4%S [3], and we assume that 40%A of returns are fit-related. Together this is $0.66D of fit-related return cost per order. If ClosetTwin cuts these returns by 15%A, the saving is $0.0996D, about $0.10 per order. This is the most the tool could cost and still break even for the retailer.
The reduction of 15% is a shop-wide figure, and it is not a modest one. We assume that 25%A of orders include a scan, so a 15% cut across all fit-related returns means about 60%D fewer fit-related returns among the scanned orders. This is above the lowest scenario of Gustafsson et al., who test reductions between 40% and 80% [4], and the largest field study that we have found shows size-finder users slightly more likely to return an item [11]. We keep the 15% because it is the effect at which the plan works, not because the evidence supports it, and the pilot must show it. The prices are set as a fraction of this value. The Growth tier charges $0.04A per order, which is 40% of the break-even value, and the Enterprise tier $0.03A, which is 30%. At these prices a retailer breaks even if the true reduction of fit-related returns is at least 6.03%D (Growth) or 4.52%D (Enterprise), and the retailer keeps the rest of the saving. Under our mix of 60% Growth and 40% Enterprise clients, the blended price is $0.036D per order, about ₪0.13 at 3.70 shekels to the dollar.

6.3 The pilot design
The claim to be tested is that a fit tool lowers the fit-related return rate, defined as orders with a size or fit return reason divided by orders. We randomize at shopper level, 50/50, and analyse by intention to treat (Table 6.2). With a significance level of 0.05 (two-sided) and 80% power, a four-week pilot at a large Israeli retailer of 600,000 orders a year detects a relative reduction of about 7.8%D. At a European retailer of one million orders a year the figure is 6.1%D. The pass line is a 10% reduction with a 95% interval above zero, set above the retailer’s break-even effect of 4.5% to 6.0% (Chapter 8). A mid-size Israeli retailer of 100,000 orders a year could detect only about 19%D. A mid-size retailer also produces only about 3,850 orders per arm in four weeks, so the planned minimum of 12,000 orders per arm is met by large retailers only. Mid-size pilots therefore either run longer or count as pooled evidence, and only large-retailer pilots are individual tests. Pooled evidence needs its own design, with the pilots analysed together and the differences between retailers reported, and we have not yet fixed it. Of the 30 Israeli candidates, only the 5 large ones can run an individual test. In Europe, most pilots are free app-store installs that have no control group. We call their results non-causal, and we do not count them toward the pass line. The need for such a test is real: the largest study in our sources found that shoppers

Operations, Team and Roadmap
7.1 Operating model
ClosetTwin runs as three parts: a capture widget on the retailer’s product page, an inference backend that turns the scan, the garment data and the stated fit preference into a size, and a data feed that returns size and return signals to the retailer. The backend runs on rented cloud capacity, so cost grows with scans and not with fixed equipment. We assume a cost to serve of $0.0075 per order, which is a scan cost of $0.03 on the 25% of orders that include a scan (both assumedA).
7.2 Team and roles
The team grows from 3 people in year 1 to 4, 5, 9 and 12 in years 2 to 5 (assumedA, at a fully loaded cost of $90,000 per person-year). The first three roles are engineering, data science and partnerships with a compliance duty attached. The ninth to twelfth positions arrive in year 4 with the Europe entry and add sales, integration support and privacy compliance. The cost of $90,000 per person-year is a blended assumption with no benchmark behind it, for Israeli engineers and European salespeople alike. The model’s operating cost is this payroll only, and it omits marketing, cloud base cost, legal review and travel (Section 9.6). We describe roles only and name no individuals. The size is deliberately lean: Israeli revenue cannot carry a larger team, as Chapter 8 shows.

9.2 Israeli privacy law, with GDPR secondary

The primary legal risk is Israel’s Privacy Protection Law as amended by Amendment 13, in force since 14 August 2025 [28]. A biometric identifier is data of special sensitivity when it is used or intended to identify a person [29], and a database holding such data about more than 100,000 people triggers a duty to notify the Authority. The mitigation is design: ClosetTwin scans for fit and never for identification, collects the minimum, and runs a consent flow and a compliance milestone before any pilot data are collected. Three limits apply. Explicit consent is our design choice, since the law defines consent as explicit or implied. Whether body measurements count as health data is an open legal question, so we treat it as a design risk and draw no conclusion. And the Authority’s data-minimization opinion is a 2021 draft [30]. Article 9 of the GDPR [32] is secondary: it applies when Israeli sites sell to EU customers and in the Europe stage.

9.3 Null pilot effect and weak conversion
A size tool can fail to cut returns. The largest study in our sources found users slightly more likely to return an item [11]. The pilot gate of Chapter 6 is the mitigation: no rollout without a pass. The assumed 50% conversion of pilots to paying clients is the weakest link of the plan, and it is named here with this risk.

9.4 Accuracy at edge sizes
A supervised size model reached 89.66% accuracy on scan data but failed at edge sizes [24]. Rare-size shoppers therefore get a fallback to the retailer’s size chart.

9.5 Platform risk, scan cost and slower Europe entry
Google announced in 2023 a generative try-on model [21], so a retailer may rely on the platform. Our answer, the combination of garment data and preference, is still a hypothesis. If a scan costs more than about $0.1 to serve, the model fails, and the

Adding them would raise the funding ask, and we did not add them because we have no sourced numbers. The model also has no churn. The deficit and the ask in Chapter 8 are therefore lower bounds.

Conclusion

10.1 The case
Returns cost retailers hundreds of billions of dollars a year, with estimates from $685 to $890 billion for 2024 [1], [2], and size and fit uncertainty is a leading cause [3]. ClosetTwin’s answer, a fit tool built on a body scan, garment data and stated preference, is a hypothesis and not a result. A four-week, shopper-level pilot can accept or reject it cheaply, and a retailer breaks even at an effect of 4.5% to 6.0%, below our 10% pass line.
10.2 Israel first, the ask and next steps
Israel’s addressable fee pool is only about $198,000 a year, so it cannot pay for the company. We choose it as a reference market because we can reach all 30 candidate retailers directly, pilots there are cheap, and a passed pilot at a large local retailer is the evidence a European buyer would ask for. That is an argument, not a proven fact, and the model does not include selling to Europe through Israeli retailers. Europe carries the revenue and enters in year 4.
We ask for about $2.8D million: the cumulative deficit of $2.33 million at the end of year 5 plus a 20% buffer. It funds the plan through year 5 only. Break-even in year 6 is an extrapolation outside the model, with a team of 12 and no churn, and Table 8.1 shows how the result moves under other assumptions. We propose to release the money in tranches tied to the pilot gate: the first for the Israeli pilots, the second for Europe only after a large-retailer pilot passes the 10% line. The plan shows no operating profit within five years. The Israeli pilots come first, after the privacy compliance milestone. If no large-retailer pilot passes, we stop and do not scale.
