1.1 The returns bill

Every order that comes back is a sale that did not finish and a cost that did. The
two best estimates of what this costs retailers in the United States are large and
differ from each other. Appriss Retail reports that total returns of the retail industry
amounted to $685 billion in merchandise in 2024, and the National Retail Federation
projects $890 billion for the same year [1], [2]. The gap of $205 billion is a difference of
method and not a contradiction. The Appriss figure is built from surveys of 150 North
American retail executives and 1,000 consumers together with data of the US Census
Bureau, while the NRF figure is a projection published on the federation's report page
[1], [2]. We therefore quote the range and not a single number, and we do not add the
two. Appriss also reports that online sales account for $362 billion of the returned
value and that fraud and abuse account for 15.14% of all returns [1]. The second figure
matters for honesty: not every return is a fit problem, and a fitting tool cannot reduce
fraud.

Two different return rates circulate in the trade press, and they must not be merged.
Appriss reports that online returns reached 24.52% of all online sales, which covers
every product category [1]. Coresight Research estimates that the average return rate
of online apparel orders was 24.4% for the twelve months ended March 2023, from a
survey of 100 decision-makers at US apparel brands and retailers [3]. The similarity of
the two numbers is a coincidence of two scopes. This plan uses the Coresight figure,
because it is the one that is specific to apparel, and it states each time that the base
is a small US survey.

1.2 Why fit is the core cause

Among the reasons for apparel returns, size and fit come first. In the Coresight survey,
53% of respondents name size or fit as the top reason for online apparel returns,
followed by color (16%) and damage (10%) [3]. This is a share of respondents and
not a share of returns, and the plan treats it that way: our own model assumes that
40% of returns are fit-related, which is a deliberate step below the survey figure
(Chapter 8). The same picture appears in the research literature. Abdulla et al. state
that online shopping yields a large percentage of returns due to size and fit [5], and
Alemany et al. describe the high number of returns and their management, logistics

and operating costs as an important economic burden for fashion companies [6].
Peng and Duraffourg report that the return rate in the textile industry is in general
between 20% and 40%, a secondary claim that they make without a citation in their
abstract [7].

The literature also tells us what kind of problem this is. A shopper who orders online
cannot try the garment on, so the decision is made on incomplete information: the
shopper knows his or her body only roughly, and the garment is described by a size
label that means different things at different brands. Alemany et al. note that earlier
fitting technologies did not reach a compromise between cost, efficacy and accuracy
that would allow wide market penetration [6], and we treat this as the historical gap
that a lower-cost scan and a data-driven model can address. In a different field, Pfaff
and Spann report that augmented reality is particularly effective for larger products
with higher spatial fit uncertainty [8]. We cite it only as an analogy: the study is about
home interiors and not about apparel.

1.3 What one return costs

A return is expensive because it is handled twice. In the one study that built the cost
of a return from transaction data, 2,229 return transactions of a Scandinavian fashion
footwear retailer, the cost of a return is approximately 17% of the prime cost, and
product handling and transportation together make up 72% of that cost [4]. This is a
single retailer and a single product category, so it is an anchor for the model and not
a market constant. Coresight estimates, for the United States, that a 24.4% return
rate on a $155.8 billion online apparel and footwear market translates to $38 billion in
returns and $25.1 billion in processing costs in 2023 [3]. We use the Gustafsson ratio
in our model and show the Coresight figures only as a statement of scale.

For Israel, which is the first market of this plan, the legal setting makes returns struc-
turally material. According to the official guide of the Israeli Consumer Protection and
Fair Trade Authority, a consumer may cancel a distance-selling transaction within
fourteen days of closing the transaction or of receiving the document with its details,
whichever is later; use of the product does not remove the right, and the business
must refund the consumer within fourteen days of receiving the cancellation notice
[9]. The business may charge a cancellation fee of 5% of the transaction or NIS 100,
whichever is lower, and may not charge it when the product is defective or differs
from the details provided [9]. We found no verified Israeli return rate. The plan's return
rate therefore stays at the US figure of 24.4%A1, and it is an assumption anchored on
a US survey and not Israeli data.

1.4 The thesis and how this plan tests it

ClosetTwin is a business-to-business virtual fitting room for fashion e-commerce.
The shopper gives a body scan from a phone, the retailer supplies the garment's
measurements, and the service combines both with the shopper's stated fit preference
to recommend a size. The claim at the center of the plan is a hypothesis and not a
fact: a fit tool built on scan, garment data and preference lowers fit-related returns
enough to pay for itself. The evidence is mixed. Gustafsson et al. model reductions of
fit-related return costs of up to 80% under assumed verification rates, but this is a
scenario [4]. The largest field study in our sources, covering 496,365 items ordered
by 75,707 customers, found that customers who used a size finder were 0.65% more
likely to return an item, although their customer lifetime value was higher [10]. A plan
that ignored the second result would be dishonest.

The plan therefore treats the effect as the quantity to be measured. Chapter 2 sizes
the market and finds that Israel alone is too small to carry the company. Chapter 3
places ClosetTwin among the existing vendors. Chapters 4 and 5 describe the product
and its pricing, which is set at a fraction of the value a retailer saves if the hypothesis
holds. Chapter 6 designs a randomized pilot that can accept or reject the hypothesis,
and Chapters 8 to 10 show what the company costs, what it earns and what it asks
for if the pilot passes. Where a number is our own, it is marked as derived or assumed,
and the legend is S sourced D derived A assumption.

2.1 Method and its limits

We size the market bottom-up, in orders and in the fee that ClosetTwin would earn on
them, and we call the result a fee pool and not a market size. A fee pool is the number
of orders a segment could send through the service multiplied by our blended price,
so it measures what we could earn and not what retailers or shoppers spend. Each
input carries one of three marks: sourced, derived by us from other inputs, or assumed
by us with a stated basis (S sourced D derived A assumption). Table 2.1 lists them all.

The Israeli figures need a warning. The market numbers come from Statista estimates
that a research report of the Knesset Research and Information Center relays [11]. They
are not a release of the Central Bureau of Statistics, the 2024 value is an estimate, and
the fashion share is the 2022 value. Publisher estimates of the virtual-fitting-room
market are context only and enter no calculation: IMARC Group reports that this global
market reached $8.5 billion in 2025 and will reach $52.6 billion by 2034, a compound
annual growth rate of 21.80% [12], but the definition and method sit in a paid report
that we could not read, and we found no second publisher that we could verify.

2.2 Israeli online commerce in context

Israeli online commerce is small next to the markets in which fitting vendors usually
operate, but it is growing. The Knesset report, citing Statista, puts total online trade at
about $6.1 billion in 2023, or about NIS 22.6 billion, which is about 2.6% of household
private consumption; the estimate for 2024 is $6.9 billion and the forecast for 2029
is $11.3 billion [11]. The ratio of the two 2023 figures gives a rate of 3.70 shekels to
the dollar, and we use this derived rate whenever a shekel equivalent is shown, for

in 2022 it was about 21.6% of Israeli online commerce, ahead of electronics at about

2.3 TAM: Israeli online fashion

For the total addressable market we take the 2022 fashion share of 21.6% and hold it
at its 2022 value for 2024, which gives $1.49 billionD of online fashion sales in Israel,
about NIS 5.5 billion. We multiply the share and not the shekel figure for 2022 because
the report's own 2022 figures imply a rate of 3.37 shekels to the dollar, and a 2022
shekel value converted at the 2023 rate would mix two years. At an assumed average
order value of $80A this is 18.6 millionD online fashion orders a year. At the blended
price of $0.036 per order, the fee pool of the whole Israeli online fashion market is
only $0.67 millionD a year.

2.4 SAM: local Israeli e-tailers only

The serviceable market is smaller still. The largest Israeli online fashion sellers are
foreign: in the 2020 sales ranking of the Knesset report, Shein, Next, ASOS and Zara
appear with $151, $96, $57 and $51 million, about $355 million together, and Temu
and AliExpress are not listed [11]. A foreign site that serves Israeli shoppers will not
buy a plug-in from an Israeli start-up for that market. We therefore assume that 45%A
of Israeli online fashion orders go to local e-tailers, which is 8.4 millionD orders. No
source sizes Israeli fashion e-tailers, so we assume three size bands: 5 large retailers
with 600,000 orders a year each, 25 mid-size retailers with 100,000 each and 250
small shops with 12,000 each. The bands add up to 8.5 million orders, within 2% of the
8.4 million above. We address the large and mid-size retailers only, since a shop with
12,000 orders pays about $430 a year and cannot justify an integration. That leaves
30 candidate retailers and 5.5 millionD orders, a fee pool of $198,000D a year.

      ISRAEL IS A REFERENCE MARKET, NOT A REVENUE MARKET

    The Israeli SAM fee pool is about $0.2 million a year. No team can live on that.
    The Israeli plan is therefore a strategy for reference customers, for pilots that
    can prove the effect, and for early learning. Revenue must come from Europe.

4.2 From scan to body model

A body model built from scan data is feasible and well studied, and the studies explain
why it is needed. Alhassawi et al. analyzed a dataset of 677 participants and found
that only 63 of them (9.15%) kept the same size across bust, waist and hip, that 614
(90.84%) fell into different sizes on different measurements, and that 35.45% were
not adequately accommodated by the existing sizing scheme [23]. In other words,
a single size label fits a minority of bodies on all dimensions at once. Alemany et al.
made the same point earlier, and noted that body-scanning technologies at that time
had not achieved a compromise between cost, efficacy and accuracy that would allow
wide market penetration [6]. That study is from 2013 and we cite it as history. What
has changed is that phone-based capture now exists: 3DLOOK states that a user can
complete a scan independently with two guided smartphone photos and no dedicated
hardware [17]. This is a vendor statement, and it does not tell us how accurate such a
scan is for apparel. We found no independent source for the accuracy of phone scans
in apparel sizing, so we state it as an engineering target that the pilot must measure
and not as a result.

4.3 Why scan alone is not enough

A body measurement does not choose a size, because the same body can want
different sizes in different garments. Peng and Duraffourg ran an experiment with
175 female participants of shapes from XS to 4XL on six garment tops. They report
that fit preference has a significant impact on the size choice, and that knowing
the three-dimensional shape of customers alone is not enough for an efficient size
recommendation tool [7]. Two further findings, which we know only from their abstracts,
point the same way. Sembium et al. report that latent-factor models that use personas
and return codes improve the area under the curve by 17 to 21% against baselines, and
that in an online A/B test they raised the percentage of "fit" transactions by 0.49% over
the control [24]. Zhang et al. report that thin models dissuade consumers with larger
clothing sizes from buying online because of perceived body-size dissimilarity and a
heightened perception of fit risk [25]. The product therefore takes three inputs and
not one: the scan, the garment's measurements and the shopper's stated preference.
That the combination beats any single input is a hypothesis of this plan, which the
pilot tests (Chapter 6).

4.4 The size model and its accuracy limits

The best evidence we hold for the model comes from a study on scan data that
used the same kind of measurements. Alhassawi et al. report that a support vector
machine that works on the primary measurements reaches an accuracy of 89.66%,
outperforming a model that adds further dimensions through principal-component

ClosetTwin  Page 13 of 39
ClosetTwin  PRODUCT AND TECHNOLOGY

analysis, which reaches 68.97% [23]. They also report that sizes 32 and 34 received no
correct predictions, which they attribute to overlapping features and too few training
samples [23]. We take two lessons from this. First, a simple model on a few good
measurements can be accurate enough to be worth testing. Second, the accuracy of
such a model is lowest at the edges of the size range, so rare sizes need a fallback, for
example a prompt to measure again or a note to the shopper that the recommendation
is uncertain. We state the 89.66% as the best evidence in hand and not as ClosetTwin's
measured performance, because the study did not use our scans, our garments or
our shoppers, and we have no measurement of our own yet.

4.5 Integration with retailer platforms

Integration follows a standard pattern. On Shopify, an app is a set of components that
the developer declares in the app configuration, and Shopify runs most of them, so
that only the backend is the developer's to host [26]. A ClosetTwin app on that model
needs a widget extension for the product page, a backend for the size model and a
feed of the retailer's garment data. We have checked this pattern only for Shopify,
which is a minor channel in Israel (Chapter 6); other platforms are not yet checked,
and the plan does not assume that integration there is equally cheap. We assume
$0.03A as the cost of one scan in our own infrastructure and 25%A of orders that
include a scan, which together give a cost to serve of $0.0075 per order (Chapter 5).

4.6 Privacy by design: scan for fit, not identification

A body scan is personal data, and the product is designed so that it is not used for
identification. Israel's Privacy Protection Law was amended by Amendment 13, pub-
lished on 14 August 2024, and the amendment takes effect one year after publication,
that is, on 14 August 2025 [27]. Under the law's definitions, a biometric identifier is
data of special sensitivity when it is used, or intended to be used, to identify a person
or to verify his or her identity in a digital manner [27], [28]. ClosetTwin scans for fit only.
The design therefore never uses body data to identify anyone, collects the minimum
that the size model needs, keeps the scan separate from any account identity and, as
a design rule, discards the images once the measurements are extracted. We offer
explicit consent as our own design choice: the law defines consent as informed con-
sent, explicit or implied [28], so we do not claim that the law requires it. Whether body
measurements for sizing could count as health data, which is a separate category of
special sensitivity [28], is an open legal question. We treat it as a design risk that the
compliance review must answer, not as a conclusion (Chapter 9).

The regulator's own documents point the same way. The Privacy Protection Authority's
2021 draft opinion, published for public hearing, states that collecting excess data
beyond what is necessary is in itself an infringement of privacy [29], and its 2025
guide to privacy-enhancing technologies says that building them into the design

ClosetTwin  Page 14 of 39
ClosetTwin  PRODUCT AND TECHNOLOGY

of systems supports compliance and serves as a foundation for building trust with
users [30]. We report this as the Authority's view. It is not evidence that retailers or
shoppers pay more for privacy. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is
secondary for this plan; it applies when Israeli sites sell to EU customers and in the
Europe stage, and it treats biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a
person as a special category [31].

4.7 Visualization: an extension, not the core

Image-based try-on is a separate research field that has been surveyed. Chen et
al. reviewed 69 research articles on the factors that influence purchasing decisions
and the adoption of virtual try-on [32], Islam et al. state that the technology has the
potential to reduce returns and to optimize conversion [33], and Song et al. survey
methods that synthesize a person image dressed in a given garment [34]. The word
to keep from this is "potential": we know these studies only by their abstracts, and
none of them shows a measured reduction in returns. The first release of ClosetTwin
therefore leaves visualization out. If the pilot succeeds, a try-on image can be added
as an extension on the same data, and Google's try-on model, which we discuss in
Chapter 3, shows that this layer is the one most exposed to platform competition.

Business Model and Pricing

5.1 Who pays for what

The retailer pays, and it pays per order served. The shopper uses the widget for free.
This follows from where the benefit lands: the saving that ClosetTwin promises is the
cost of the returns that no longer happen, and that saving grows with the number of
orders the retailer processes. A fee per order therefore moves with the value delivered,
it lets a retailer start small, and it avoids a fixed license that a retailer would have to
justify before it has seen any effect. The only other revenue in the model is the fixed
fee for the pilot. The data feed described in Chapter 4 earns nothing in this plan.

5.2 Value-based price

The price is set against the value, and the value is the return cost that the tool avoids.
We follow the logic of Gustafsson et al., who compute what a fitting technology may
cost per unit before it stops paying for itself. In their case study of a footwear retailer,
with a prime cost assumed at EUR 30, the allowed cost is EUR 1.1 per pair if the
technology verifies fit in 40% of cases, EUR 1.6 at 60% and EUR 2.1 at 80% [4]. Those
figures belong to one retailer and one category, so we rebuild the calculation with our
own inputs; formula (8.1) in Chapter 8 gives it symbol by symbol. The cost of a return
is 17%S of the prime cost [4]. With an $80A order and a prime cost that we assume
to be 50%A of it, the prime cost is $40D and a return costs $6.80D. The return rate
is 24.4%S [3], and we assume that 40%A of returns are fit-related. Together this is
$0.66D of fit-related return cost per order. If ClosetTwin cuts these returns by 15%A,
the saving is $0.0996D, about $0.10 per order. This is the most the tool could cost and
still break even for the retailer.

ClosetTwin                  Page 16 of 39
ClosetTwin                                                               BUSINESS MODEL AND PRICING

The reduction of 15% is deliberately modest. Gustafsson et al. test reductions between
40% and 80% [4], but the largest field study that we have found shows size-finder
users slightly more likely to return an item [10], so we start well below the scenarios
and let the pilot decide. The prices are set as a fraction of this value. The Growth tier
charges $0.04A per order, which is 40% of the break-even value, and the Enterprise
tier $0.03A, which is 30%. At these prices a retailer breaks even if the true reduction of
fit-related returns is at least 6.03%D (Growth) or 4.52%D (Enterprise), and the retailer
keeps the rest of the saving. Under our mix of 60% Growth and 40% Enterprise clients,
the blended price is $0.036D per order, about 0.13 at 3.70 shekels to the dollar.

5.5 Unit economics and the scan-cost condition

6.3 The pilot design

The claim to be tested is that a fit tool lowers the fit-related return rate, defined as
orders with a size or fit return reason divided by orders. We randomize at shopper level,

95% interval above zero, set above the retailer's break-even effect of 4.5% to 6.0%

about 19%D. A mid-size retailer also produces only about 3,850 orders per arm in four
weeks, so the planned minimum of 12,000 orders per arm is met by large retailers
only. Mid-size pilots therefore either run longer or count as pooled evidence, and only

7.1 Operating model

ClosetTwin runs as three parts: a capture widget on the retailer's product page, an
inference backend that turns the scan, the garment data and the stated fit preference
into a size, and a data feed that returns size and return signals to the retailer. The
backend runs on rented cloud capacity, so cost grows with scans and not with fixed
equipment. We assume a cost to serve of $0.0075 per order, which is a scan cost of
$0.03 on the 25% of orders that include a scan (both assumedA).

7.2 Team and roles

The team grows from 3 people in year 1 to 4, 5, 9 and 12 in years 2 to 5 (assumedA, at a
fully loaded cost of $90,000 per person-year). The first three roles are engineering,
data science and partnerships with a compliance duty attached. The ninth to twelfth
positions arrive in year 4 with the Europe entry and add sales, integration support and
privacy compliance. The cost of $90,000 per person-year is a blended assumption
with no benchmark behind it, for Israeli engineers and European salespeople alike.
The model's operating cost is this payroll only, and it omits marketing, cloud base cost,
legal review and travel (Section 9.6). We describe roles only and name no individuals.
The size is deliberately lean: Israeli revenue cannot carry a larger team, as Chapter 8
shows.

 = 24.4% from a US survey [3],  = $6.80 (17% of an assumed prime cost of $40),
 = 40% and  = 15%, the last three assumedA inputs. The 17% comes from one
footwear retailer, where it is the return cost per pair sold, not per return [4]. Because
only a share of pairs is returned, the cost of one return in that study is higher than 17%
of prime cost. Using $6.80 per return is therefore a conservative lower bound: a higher
cost per return would raise the retailer's saving. Prime cost is also our own assumption
of 50% of the order value, not the retailer's purchase price as in the source. These
inputs give be = $0.0996D per order. The blended price of $0.036 (about 0.13) is
36% of that value.

The reduction  = 15% applies to the whole shop. Only 25% of orders include a scan
(an assumedA share), so a 15% shop-wide reduction means about a 60% reduction
of fit-related returns among scanned orders, if the tool acts only on those orders.
That is above the 40% low scenario of Gustafsson et al. and cannot be called modest.
The pilot has to show it. The model also bills every order of a client, not only scanned
orders. If billing covered scanned orders only, revenue would fall in proportion, and
we flag that the billing base must be fixed in the contract.

8.2 Five-year projection

Figure 8.1 shows the model output. Revenue grows from $10,000 in year 1 to
$782,000D in year 5, of which Israeli subscriptions are only $54,000. The operating
result is negative in every year: $-272,000, $-347,750, $-423,500, $-771,375 and
$-517,750. The cumulative deficit is $2,332,375D at the end of year 5. There is no
operating profit within the five-year horizon. The funding ask is the cumulative deficit
plus a 20% buffer (assumedA), which is $2,798,850D, or about $2.8 million. It funds
the plan through year 5 only.

Year 6 is an extrapolation outside the model, not a result. A hand calculation shows
that run-rate break-even needs about 37 European paying clients, and that the plan
could reach that number in year 6, but only if the team stays at 12, no client leaves and
payroll and pilot costs are the only costs. None of these holds by construction. The
model has no churn, and it leaves out cost lines a real company would carry, among
them marketing and customer acquisition, cloud base cost, legal and compliance
review, travel and general administration. We list them as a limitation rather than
add numbers we cannot source. The scan cost of $0.03 and the person-year cost of
$90,000 are assumptions with no benchmark behind them.

Three limits are stated openly. First, Israel's addressable fee pool is only about
$198,000 a year (Chapter 2), so Europe carries the revenue. Of the $782,000 in year 5,
$648,000 comes from one chain of assumptions: 18 European clients, each averaging
one million orders a year, each paying for its whole first year. The one million orders
per client has no source. Billing for the whole first year is a simplification, since a
nine-month sales cycle and a pilot would delay the first invoice. Second, the 50%
conversion of pilots to paying clients is assumed, and the Israeli path converts at
only about 35% (Chapter 6). Third, Table 8.1 shows how the company's result changes
when conversion, orders per client and the year of Europe entry change, so a reader
can see that the plan does not rest on one case.

Table 8.1 shows that every change moves the ask up and none brings break-even
inside the horizon. Conversion at 35% lifts the ask from $2,798,850 to $3,027,135 and
cuts year-5 revenue from $782,000 to $571,400. Half the orders per European client
gives $458,000 of revenue and an ask of $3,106,650. A one-year delay of Europe is
the worst case: year-5 revenue falls to $104,000 and the ask rises to $3,423,450.
Doubling the scan cost raises the ask to $3,028,620 and leaves revenue unchanged.

9.2 Israeli privacy law, with GDPR secondary

The primary legal risk is Israel's Privacy Protection Law as amended by Amendment 13,
in force since 14 August 2025 [27]. A biometric identifier is data of special sensitivity
when it is used or intended to identify a person [28], and a database holding such
data about more than 100,000 people triggers a duty to notify the Authority. The
mitigation is design: ClosetTwin scans for fit and never for identification, collects the
minimum, and runs a consent flow and a compliance milestone before any pilot data
are collected. Three limits apply. Explicit consent is our design choice, since the law
defines consent as explicit or implied. Whether body measurements count as health

ClosetTwin                                                                                Page 29 of 39
ClosetTwin  RISKS

data is an open legal question, so we treat it as a design risk and draw no conclusion.
And the Authority's data-minimization opinion is a 2021 draft [29]. Article 9 of the
GDPR [31] is secondary: it applies when Israeli sites sell to EU customers and in the
Europe stage.

9.3 Null pilot effect and weak conversion

A size tool can fail to cut returns. The largest study in our sources found users slightly
more likely to return an item [10]. The pilot gate of Chapter 6 is the mitigation: no
rollout without a pass. The assumed 50% conversion of pilots to paying clients is the
weakest link of the plan, and it is named here with this risk.

9.4 Accuracy at edge sizes

A supervised size model reached 89.66% accuracy on scan data but failed at edge
sizes [23]. Rare-size shoppers therefore get a fallback to the retailer's size chart.

9.5 Platform risk, scan cost and slower Europe entry

Google offers free try-on [20], so a retailer may rely on the platform. Our answer, the
combination of garment data and preference, is still a hypothesis. If a scan costs
more than about $0.1 to serve, the model fails, and the public price lists of one scan
vendor [17] range from $1.50 per extra request (FitXpress Pro tier, a health and fitness
scan) to about $2.00 per scan (Mobile Tailor Premium tier, $999 for 500 scans, a
made-to-measure product). That is 50 to 67 timesD our assumed $0.03, so we need
an in-house scan, and the $0.03 itself has no engineering benchmark yet. Last, nearly
all revenue depends on Europe entry, because Israel's fee pool is about $198,000 a
year. If entry is slower than planned, the funding ask rises.

9.6 Limits of the register

The register covers the risks we could name, but the cost base is incomplete. The
model has payroll, cost to serve and pilot costs, and it omits marketing and customer
acquisition, cloud base cost, legal and compliance review, travel and general adminis-
tration. Adding them would raise the funding ask, and we did not add them because
we have no sourced numbers. The model also has no churn. The deficit and the ask in
Chapter 8 are therefore lower bounds.

Conclusion

10.1 The case

Returns cost retailers hundreds of billions of dollars a year, with estimates from
$685 to $890 billion for 2024 [1], [2], and size and fit uncertainty is a leading cause [3].
ClosetTwin's answer, a fit tool built on a body scan, garment data and stated preference,
is a hypothesis and not a result. Its strength is that a four-week, shopper-level pilot
can accept or reject it cheaply, and a retailer breaks even at an effect of 4.5% to 6.0%,
below the 10% pass line we set.

10.2 Why Israel first, and the ask

Israel's addressable fee pool is only about $198,000 a year, so it cannot pay for the
company. We choose it as a reference market for three reasons: the team can reach
all 30 candidate retailers directly, a pilot there is cheap to run, and a passed pilot at a
large local retailer is the evidence a European buyer would ask for. That last point is an
argument and not a proven fact, and the model does not include the route of selling
to Europe through Israeli retailers. Europe carries the revenue and enters in year 4.

We ask for about $2.8D million: the cumulative deficit of $2.33 million at the end of year
5 plus a 20% buffer. The ask funds the plan through year 5 only. Break-even in year 6
is an extrapolation outside the model, with a team of 12 and no churn, and Table 8.1
shows how the result moves when conversion, orders per client and Europe entry
change. We propose to release the money in tranches tied to the pilot gate: the first
for the Israeli pilots, the second for Europe only after a large-retailer pilot passes the
10% line. The plan shows no operating profit within five years, and we say so plainly.

10.3 Next steps

The Israeli pilots come first, after the privacy compliance milestone and before any
pilot data are collected. If no large-retailer pilot passes, we stop and do not scale.
